You probably already have people to talk to.

Friends. Colleagues. Mentors. Partners. A professional network.

They matter.

But when something important is at stake, who gives you the “what about?”

Who questions the assumption everyone else has accepted?

Who helps you see the decision differently?

Who can stress-test your thinking without having a vested interest in what you choose?

That is the role Concentric is designed to put into the room.

A strong network can still leave a gap.

Different relationships do different jobs.

  • A friend knows your history.
  • A partner may live with the consequences of what you choose.
  • A colleague understands the system you are operating inside.
  • A mentor brings experience and advice.
  • A sponsor has invested in your progression.
  • A professional network can create access, information and opportunity.

None of those relationships needs replacing.

But they do not always create the conditions for candid, independent challenge.

Especially when

  • the question is still unfinished;
  • you already know what you want and need the plan stress-tested;
  • several options look plausible;
  • you are questioning a path that has worked well until now;
  • seniority, reputation or organisational politics make candor harder;
  • encouragement is useful, but no longer enough.

Concentric creates a different kind of professional room.

Six women. One deliberately composed Council.

Women are placed around what they are navigating, not simply what they do.

The aim is not to assemble six identical careers around a table.

It is to create enough shared context to understand one another — and enough difference to interrupt habitual thinking.

Each Council has

  • six women;
  • 90 minutes once a month on Zoom;
  • a neutral facilitator who holds time and structure;
  • shared Council principles;
  • continuity across sessions;
  • private reflection before and after the room.

The founder is not the facilitator.

The value has to exist in the Council itself.

Bring the question before you have answered it.

You do not need a perfectly articulated problem.

In fact, some of the most useful questions may still be unfinished.

You might be

  • Considering a role or career change.
  • Facing redundancy, re-entry or a new chapter.
  • Leaving a successful path for something different.
  • Clear on the destination but uncertain about the route.
  • Questioning an assumption that has shaped your professional life for years.
  • Trying to work out whether an opportunity is actually right for you.
  • Holding a decision that everyone around you seems to have an opinion about.
  • Or simply noticing that something no longer fits — before you know what comes next.

Concentric is for professionally established women at an inflection point.

Not defined by title, industry or generation.

The commonality is that something consequential is in play.

What happens in the room?

You bring the decision, question, tension or possibility that is occupying your thinking.

The other five women are not there to tell you what to do.

They are there to

  • Listen carefully.
  • Ask better questions.
  • Challenge an assumption.
  • Introduce another interpretation.
  • Notice what you may have discounted.
  • Give you the “what about?”

Sometimes the useful thing will be agreement.

Sometimes it will be disagreement.

Sometimes someone will surface an option you had not considered.

Sometimes the conversation will make you less certain than when you arrived.

That can still be movement.

The point is not to leave every Council with an answer. The point is to leave having thought differently because the room existed.

Movement, not just a good conversation.

Concentric is not designed around upward advancement as the default measure of progress.

Movement can look smaller, stranger or more consequential than that.

  • A real question becomes clearer.
  • An assumption is challenged.
  • A new option appears.
  • A decision changes.
  • A plan survives being stress-tested.
  • Action is taken.
  • Action is consciously declined.
  • Something that felt fixed becomes open again.
  • Or you leave with a better question than the one you brought in.

We are interested in what productive challenge changes — not simply whether the conversation felt good.

Your professional devil’s advocate.

“Devil’s advocate” does not mean arguing for the sake of it.

It does not mean criticism.

And it does not mean creating a room where the loudest person wins.

At Concentric, productive challenge should sit alongside trust, curiosity, confidentiality, difference and care.

The role is simpler

  • To ask the question that might otherwise go unasked.
  • To question the assumption everyone else has accepted.
  • To help someone stress-test important thinking before it hardens into a decision.
  • To create space for the “what about?”

That is the professional role Concentric is interested in making more available.

Not another network.

  • Concentric is not built around collecting contacts.
  • It is not a networking event.
  • It is not coaching.
  • It is not therapy.
  • It is not a community built around constant participation.
  • It is not founder access, famous logos or proximity to a particular seniority tier.

The model is deliberately small.

Six women.

One recurring Council.

A professionally significant question.

Enough continuity for the room to become more useful over time.

Why strangers?

Because distance can be useful.

The women in your Council do not work inside your organisation.

They are not your oldest friends.

They have not spent years reinforcing a particular version of who you are.

They do not need your decision to work for them.

That does not automatically make strangers objective, nor does it guarantee useful challenge.

That is exactly what Edition One is testing.

Can previously unconnected women develop enough trust, virtually, to bring increasingly consequential and unfinished questions into the room?

Can difference produce useful challenge rather than noise?

Can that challenge create movement worth continuing?

Those are questions we intend to learn from, not assumptions we intend to disguise as fact.

Why six?

Small enough for depth.

Large enough for difference.

Six gives each Council multiple perspectives without turning the room into an audience.

It also creates a meaningful constraint: every seat matters.

We are testing whether six women, 90 minutes and a monthly rhythm is the right architecture.

Edition One is designed to help answer that.

Why a facilitator?

The facilitator does not coach the Council.

She does not provide the answer.

She does not become the centre of the experience.

Her role is to hold the conditions around the conversation:

  • time
  • structure
  • participation
  • continuity
  • and the shared principles of the room

The thinking belongs to the Council.

Before the Council

A few private prompts help you arrive with something real to bring into the room.

Not homework. Not an assessment. Not a coaching intake.

A chance to notice:

  • Why am I here now?
  • What is occupying my thinking?
  • What might I be assuming?
  • What do I want this room to challenge?
  • What question is still unfinished?

Your reflections remain yours.

After the Council

The conversation does not need to become an accountability programme.

But it should not disappear the moment Zoom closes either.

A short private reflection helps you capture what shifted while it is still fresh.

  • What changed in my thinking?
  • What am I seeing differently?
  • Is there anything I now want to do — or consciously not do?
  • What might get in the way?

Over time, those reflections can become a record of how your thinking moved across the Council.

Edition One

The first Concentric Councils.

Edition One is a pilot.

We are not treating it as proof that the model works.

We are testing it.

We want to understand

  • whether women find the Council genuinely valuable;
  • whether deliberately composed strangers can develop sufficient trust virtually;
  • whether productive challenge actually occurs;
  • whether members begin bringing increasingly consequential or unfinished questions;
  • whether the experience changes thinking or creates movement;
  • whether women want to continue;
  • whether six women, 90 minutes and a monthly rhythm is the right architecture;
  • what role facilitation should play;
  • and what persists beyond the facilitated sessions.

Edition One founding members are helping us test the model properly.

Not simply experience a finished one.

The Evidence Base

Concentric began with a question.

What is missing from the professional lives of women who may already have careers, networks, mentors and people around them?

Our first research involved 67 women in the United States.

That work has since been supplemented by published evidence, conversations with academics and researchers, external practitioner perspectives, placement conversations and input from professionally established women.

We are interested in where those perspectives converge.

Where they do not.

And where our original assumptions need challenging.

Edition One becomes another part of that Evidence Base.

Not because a pilot will prove the model.

Because it will give us something much more useful: evidence from what actually happens in the room.
